    Size is decided against your frame

    Implant type, profile and volume are chosen from your measurements, your tissue and how much skin there is to cover it — not from a cup size or a photograph of someone else.

  2. 02

    A small incision, hidden in the fold

    Usually in the crease beneath the breast, sometimes around the areola. Both settle into a line that already exists.

  3. 03

    A pocket is made under the muscle

    Sub-muscular placement in most cases. The muscle gives the implant a layer of cover, which softens the upper edge and makes it far less likely to be visible or palpable.

  4. 04

    The implant is placed and checked

    Positioned, then assessed sitting up as well as lying down, because that is how it will be seen.

  5. 05

    Self-dissolving sutures and tape. Drains are usually unnecessary here. A surgical bra goes on to control swelling and hold position while the pocket settles.

An implant adds volume. It does not lift.

This is the single most common misunderstanding in breast surgery. If the breast has dropped, a larger implant makes a larger dropped breast — it fills the skin without raising the nipple. Where there is real sagging, the honest answer is a lift, with or without an implant, and it is a bigger operation with a longer scar. He will tell you which one you are.

A cup size is not a measurement.

It varies by brand and it describes a bra, not a body. Implants are chosen in cubic centimetres against your chest width, your tissue and your skin — which is why he will not promise you a letter, and why anyone who does is guessing.

Five things he checks.

The same five he works through in the room. A surgeon who operates on everyone who asks is not selecting for results.

Volume is what is missing

Likely a candidateWanting fullness or proportion restored, after pregnancy, weight loss, or simply because it was never there.

He’ll ask you to waitIf the breast has dropped and the nipple sits low. Volume alone will not raise it, and a bigger implant makes it heavier.

Enough tissue to cover it

Likely a candidateSkin and breast tissue that can drape over an implant. Under-muscle placement helps considerably where cover is thin.

He’ll ask you to waitIf the tissue is very thin and the expectation is large. The edge shows, and it shows more over time.

A stable point in your life

Likely a candidateFinished with pregnancy and breastfeeding, or content that the result may change if you are not.

He’ll ask you to waitIf a pregnancy is close. Breastfeeding and the volume changes around it can alter the result.

Four to six weeks of sleeping on your back

Likely a candidateSide sleeping risks displacing an implant before the pocket has settled. It is a genuine imposition and worth knowing in advance.

He’ll ask you to waitIf that is impossible for you right now. Better to plan it than to discover it.

Nicotine-free

Likely a candidateNot smoking, not vaping. Healing around a foreign body is where infection risk concentrates.

He’ll ask you to waitIf you are still smoking. An infected implant usually has to come out and go back in months later.

Before you decide.

An implant is a device, and devices do not last forever. Every item below is a genuine possibility, not fine print.

This is not a one-off operation.

Implants are not lifetime devices. At some point — years away, but not never — they will need replacing or removing, and that is a second operation with a second cost. Anyone who lets you believe otherwise has left out the part that matters most for a decision you are making in your thirties.
  • Capsular contractureScar tissue tightening around the implant, making the breast firm, high or distorted. The commonest reason a good result needs revising, and it can happen years later.
  • RuptureImplants can fail. Silicone rupture is often silent, which is why imaging is recommended periodically rather than only when something feels wrong.
  • InfectionAround a foreign body, infection usually means removing the implant and replacing it months later.
  • Rippling or a visible edgeMore likely where tissue cover is thin, and more likely over time as tissue changes.
  • Changes in nipple sensationIncreased, reduced, or occasionally lost. Usually temporary; sometimes not.
  • AsymmetryTwo breasts were never identical before surgery and will not be after. The aim is balance, not symmetry.
  • Effects on breastfeedingMost people can still breastfeed after augmentation, but it cannot be guaranteed, and incision choice is part of that conversation.

What it costs

Every quote is built from the same things. Ask for them itemised — and ask what a replacement would cost in ten years, because that is part of the real price.

  • Implant type — silicone or saline, smooth or textured
  • Whether a lift is needed as well
  • Operating time and anaesthesia
  • Facility fee
  • Garments and the follow-up schedule
  • The manufacturer’s warranty, and what it actually covers

Still asked, often

Do I need a lift as well?

It depends where your nipple sits relative to the crease beneath the breast. Below it, an implant alone will not raise things and a lift is the honest answer. He will show you on your own measurements rather than describe it.

Over or under the muscle?

Usually under. The muscle gives an extra layer of cover, which softens the upper edge and makes rippling far less likely — particularly if your own tissue is thin.

How long do implants last?

There is no expiry date, but they are not lifetime devices. Plan on needing something done eventually rather than assuming you will not.

Where does the scar go?

Usually in the crease under the breast, sometimes around the areola. Both sit in a line that already exists, and both fade substantially within a year.

Why do they look too high at first?

Because they are. The muscle is tight and the pocket has not relaxed. They drop and soften over weeks to a few months, and week two is when almost everyone panics unnecessarily.

Can I still breastfeed?

Most people can, but it cannot be promised. If it matters to you, say so at the consultation — it influences where the incision goes.
