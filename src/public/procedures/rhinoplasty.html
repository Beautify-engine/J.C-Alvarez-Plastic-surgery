One operation, five moves.

Ninety minutes to two and a half hours, longer if the septum is being straightened at the same time. Millimetres of cartilage decide the whole face.

01 / 05

Or hear him explain it

  1. 01

    The approach is chosen

    Closed, with every incision hidden inside the nostril, or open, with one fine cut across the columella between them. Open gives direct sight of the framework; closed leaves nothing visible at all. Which one depends on what has to be changed, not on preference.

  2. 02

    Bone and cartilage are reshaped

    A hump is taken down, a wide bridge narrowed, a deviated septum straightened. This is a framework operation — the skin is a covering that drapes over whatever is built underneath.

  3. 03

    The tip is refined

    Cartilage is reshaped and stitched rather than simply cut away. Removing tip support is what makes a nose look pinched and collapse years later; rebuilding it is what keeps the result stable.

  4. 04

    Breathing is checked, not assumed

    The septum and the internal valve are assessed and corrected in the same operation. A nose that looks better and works worse is a failed operation, however it photographs.

  5. 05

    Cast, then tapes

    A cast for two weeks to hold the bones while they set, then tapes for two more, then tapes at night out to eight weeks. Swelling is managed for far longer than most people expect.

The nose you see at three months is not the one you keep.

This is the slowest result in plastic surgery. Significant swelling takes four to eight weeks to settle, residual swelling runs six to nine months, and the final shape is somewhere between nine and twelve. The tip is last, always. Anyone showing you a two-week reveal is showing you swelling.

Your skin decides how sharp the result can be.

Thin skin shows every millimetre of the framework underneath, for better and worse. Thick skin drapes over refinement and mutes it, and holds swelling far longer. It is not something surgery changes, and a surgeon who does not raise it before operating is managing your expectations too late.

Five things he checks.

The same five he works through in the room. A surgeon who operates on everyone who asks is not selecting for results.

One or two things that bother you

Likely a candidateA hump, a wide or bulbous tip, a deviation. Specific complaints get specific answers.

He’ll ask you to waitIf you cannot name what you want changed, or you bring a photograph of somebody else. Their nose belongs to their face.

A nose that also has to work

Likely a candidateBlocked breathing, a deviated septum, collapse on one side. Function and shape are corrected together, in one operation.

He’ll ask you to waitNever for breathing alone without discussing shape — changing the framework changes the airway whether you planned it or not.

Skin that will show the work

Likely a candidateModerate skin thickness lets refinement read through. It is the single biggest predictor of how defined a result can be.

He’ll ask you to waitIf your skin is very thick. Definition is muted and swelling lasts longer — he will say so before, not after.

A year of patience

Likely a candidateUnderstanding that the cast comes off at two weeks and the nose keeps changing for the next twelve months.

He’ll ask you to waitIf you have a wedding in three months. Bruising goes quickly; the tip does not.

Grown, and nicotine-free

Likely a candidateFacial growth complete, in good health, not smoking.

He’ll ask you to waitIf you are still smoking. Nicotine compromises the blood supply to a skin envelope that has just been lifted off its framework.

Before you decide.

The most revised operation in plastic surgery, and the least forgiving. Every item below is a genuine possibility, not fine print.

Roughly one in ten will want a revision.

Published revision rates for primary rhinoplasty sit around five to fifteen per cent across the literature — higher than any other procedure on this site. It is not a sign of poor surgery; it is what operating on millimetres of cartilage under a skin envelope that heals unpredictably actually costs. Ask what a revision would involve, and what it would cost, before you book the first one.
  • An outcome that needs revisingThe commonest risk here by a distance. Revision is usually minor, and usually waits a full year for the tissue to settle.
  • Breathing that gets worseNarrowing a nose narrows an airway. Preventing it is the reason function is assessed and corrected in the same operation, not afterwards.
  • AsymmetryNo nose was symmetrical before surgery, and healing is not symmetrical either. Small differences are expected.
  • Numbness at the tipCommon for months. Usually resolves; occasionally patches remain.
  • Prolonged and uneven swellingSix to nine months, and the tip holds it longest. Thick skin holds it longer still.
  • A visible columellar scarOn an open approach only, a fine line between the nostrils. It fades to near-invisible in most people, but it is permanent.
  • Late collapse or irregularityCartilage can warp or lose support over years, particularly where too much was removed rather than reshaped and stitched.

What it costs

Every quote is built from the same things. Ask for them itemised — and ask specifically about revision policy, because on this operation that is not a hypothetical.

  • Primary or revision — revisions cost more and take longer
  • Whether a septoplasty is included
  • Whether cartilage grafting is needed, and where it is taken from
  • Operating time and anaesthesia
  • Facility fee
  • What a revision would cost, and who pays for it

Still asked, often

Open or closed — which is better?

Neither. Open gives direct sight of the framework and leaves a fine scar between the nostrils; closed leaves nothing visible but works less directly. What has to be changed decides it.

Will it fix my breathing?

If the obstruction is structural — a deviated septum, a collapsing valve — that is corrected in the same operation. Allergy and congestion are medical problems and surgery will not touch them.

Why does it take a year?

Skin has been lifted off a framework and has to re-drape onto a new one. That takes months, and the tip — where the skin is thickest and least mobile — is always last.

Will it look like someone else’s nose?

It should not. A nose that works on one face rarely works on another, because proportion is relative to everything around it. Bring what bothers you rather than a photograph of a stranger.

Can it be done without surgery?

Filler can camouflage a small hump or a slight asymmetry temporarily. It adds volume to a nose, so it cannot make one smaller, and it carries its own rare but serious vascular risks in this area.

When can I wear glasses again?

Not resting on the bridge while the bones are setting. Ask at the consultation how long, and plan contacts or a taped workaround for that period.
